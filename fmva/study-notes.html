Building a 3-Statement Model · core

Three-statement linkage

A three-statement model is not three documents. It is one balance sheet, observed twice, with the income statement and cash flow statement explaining the entire difference between the two observations.

That sentence is the whole engine. Everything below is the mechanics of making it true in Excel. If you can hold it, the model stops being a memorised web of links and becomes a single constraint you can reason from: every change in a balance-sheet line, between period t−1 and t, must be explained by a line on the income statement or the cash flow statement. Anything unexplained is why your model doesn't balance.

01The linkage ledger

Learn it as flows between statements, not as cell references. The direction matters more than the address.

Why the cash flow statement is a derived document, never an input

In a built model you never forecast the cash flow statement. You forecast the income statement and the balance-sheet drivers (days receivable, days payable, inventory days, capex, debt paydown), and the cash flow statement falls out of them as an arithmetic reconciliation. This is the opposite of how the statement is usually taught, and it's the thing candidates most often get backwards in a modelling test.

The consequence is diagnostic: if your balance sheet doesn't balance, the bug is almost never on the cash flow statement itself — it's a balance-sheet line whose movement isn't being carried through to CF, or is being carried through twice.

02The balance check, and what it actually tests

The check Total assets − (Total liabilities + Equity) = 0 looks like a formatting nicety. It isn't. It is a live proof that every balance-sheet movement in the period has exactly one cash-flow explanation. Build it as a row that runs across every forecast period, and give it conditional formatting — you want it screaming in the second you break it, not three schedules later.

Two failure modes people confuse

An off-by-a-constant break (the same error in every period) is usually a historical hardcode or a missing opening-balance link. An error that grows each period is a flow problem — a balance-sheet item moving without a corresponding cash-flow line, compounding forward. Diagnose by looking at the shape of the break across the row before you touch a single formula.

Diagnostic sequence when the balance sheet breaks
  1. Read the break across the row. Constant → hardcode/opening balance. Growing → a flow.
  2. Take the first period that breaks and note the magnitude — the number itself is usually recognisable (it equals capex, or the dividend, or the debt repayment).
  3. Check sign conventions on working capital. An increase in receivables is a use of cash. Most models are built with all CF items entered as positives and the formula subtracting; mixing the two conventions in one schedule is the single most common cause.
  4. Confirm every balance-sheet line has a driver: walk the balance sheet top to bottom and name the cash-flow line that moves it. Any line without one is your bug.
  5. Confirm ending cash on the CF equals the cash line on the BS — that link is often the one broken by an inserted row.
  6. Confirm dividends hit retained earnings and financing, once each.

03Circularity: interest ↔ debt ↔ cash

This is where a working model stops being a linear chain. Interest expense depends on the debt balance; the debt balance depends on how much cash is available to sweep; available cash depends on net income; net income depends on interest expense. Excel calls this a circular reference and, by default, refuses.

The standard resolutions, and when each is right:

ApproachWhat it doesUse when
Iterative calculation onExcel solves the loop by repeated substitution (File → Options → Formulas, ~100 iterations, 0.001 change)Your own model, and you understand the failure mode below
Opening-balance interestCharge interest on the beginning debt balance only — breaks the loop entirelyTimed modelling tests, models shared widely, anything auditable
Circuit breakerA toggle cell that forces interest to zero, flushing #REF!/#VALUE! cascades out of the loopAlways, if you turn iteration on
The call I'd make

Know how to build the iterative version — FMVA teaches it and it's the technically correct treatment when debt moves materially during the year. But in a live modelling test under time pressure, use opening-balance interest and say out loud that you're doing it to avoid circularity. Interviewers read that as judgment, not as a shortcut. A model that returns a number beats an elegant one full of zeros because the iteration blew up.

Average vs. opening balance — the actual trade-off

Interest on the average of opening and closing debt is more accurate when the balance changes a lot within the period: it approximates the fact that the company held the debt for only part of the year. Interest on the opening balance overstates interest in a deleveraging year and understates it in a year of new issuance.

The accuracy gain is real but usually second-order — on a company paying 6% on $500m of debt that amortises $50m in the year, the difference is roughly 6% × $25m ≈ $1.5m of pre-tax expense. Weigh that against a model that can silently produce a wrong answer if the iteration doesn't converge. In a debt-heavy context (an LBO), the average-balance treatment earns its keep and you build the circuit breaker properly. In a plain operating model, it usually doesn't.

04The cash sweep and the revolver plug

Cash cannot go negative. Once the model is closed, that constraint has to be enforced by something, and that something is the revolver: if the cash flow statement would drive cash below a minimum operating balance, the revolver draws the shortfall; if there's surplus cash above that minimum, it repays the revolver, then sweeps against term debt if the credit agreement requires it.

Two things bankers care about that FMVA underplays:

  • The minimum cash balance is an assumption, not zero. Real businesses need working cash. Setting it to zero flatters free cash flow and understates the revolver.
  • The plug must be the revolver, never cash and never equity. If your model balances because you plugged a hardcode into cash or "other assets," you have a model that balances and lies. This is a specific, fatal tell in a modelling test.

05The walkthrough you will be asked

"Depreciation increases by $10. Walk me through the three statements." The point of the question is not arithmetic — it's whether you understand that the three statements are one system. Use a 25% tax rate unless you're given one; state the rate you're using.

StatementLineΔReasoning
Income statementD&A+10The change itself
Pre-tax income−10D&A is an expense
Net income−7.5−10 × (1 − 25%); taxes fall by 2.5
Cash flowNI, then add back D&A−7.5 +10D&A never left the business
Ending cash+2.5The entire effect is the tax saved
Balance sheetCash+2.5From the CF statement
PP&E, net−10Accumulated depreciation rises
Retained earnings−7.5Assets −7.5 = equity −7.5. Balances.

The line that separates a good answer from a passable one: "cash is up 2.5, which is the depreciation tax shield — $10 of deduction at a 25% rate." Say that, and you've shown you know why, not just what.

Drill · Same question, but the company is loss-making and has no taxable income. What changes?

There is no tax shield to capture, so cash doesn't move at all. Net income falls the full 10, D&A of 10 is added back, cash is flat. On the balance sheet, PP&E −10 and retained earnings −10. It still balances, but the +2.5 is gone.

The follow-up, if they're pushing: the deduction isn't destroyed, it becomes part of a net operating loss carryforward — a deferred tax asset that shields cash taxes in a future profitable year, subject to the relevant limitation rules. That's where an interviewer testing depth is heading.

Drill · Why can't you build the model by forecasting the cash flow statement directly?

Because the cash flow statement has no independent drivers. Every line on it is a restatement of something already forecast on the income statement or implied by a balance-sheet assumption. Forecasting it directly means forecasting the same quantity twice, from two places, with nothing forcing the two to agree — and the balance sheet then can't close except by a plug.

Why this matters for M&A specifically

The three-statement model is not a deliverable in banking — it's the engine underneath the ones that are. An accretion/dilution analysis needs the target's and acquirer's income statements linked through a pro-forma balance sheet with new debt, new intangibles and their amortisation. An LBO needs the full debt schedule and cash sweep running off exactly these links, over five to seven years. A DCF needs unlevered free cash flow, which is assembled from IS and BS lines — not lifted from the cash flow statement.

Practically: the reason analysts can build a working operating model in a morning is that the linkage is muscle memory, and their attention goes to the assumptions. Get the mechanics automatic now and the judgment is what you're left thinking about.

What a banker's model adds on top of the FMVA template
  • Quarterly stubs and calendarisation. Deals close mid-year; targets have non-calendar fiscal year-ends. You need the model to produce a stub period and to restate a June year-end onto a December basis to compare against comps.
  • LTM columns. Last-twelve-months figures = last full year + current stub − prior-year stub. Every trading multiple you quote is built on this.
  • A real debt schedule. Multiple tranches, different rates and amortisation, mandatory vs. optional prepayment, cash sweep percentages, and covenant tests calculated off the model rather than typed in.
  • Scenario switching. One driver cell moving the whole model between management, base and downside cases (CHOOSE or INDEX, not IF chains).
  • Formatting as a language. Blue for hardcoded inputs, black for formulas, green for links from another sheet. Not decoration — it's how the next person audits your work in thirty seconds, and it's noticed in a test.

"Why exactly does average-balance interest create a circular reference, when opening-balance interest doesn't?"

Because of what each formula reads. Opening-balance interest reads last period's closing debt — a number that is already final before this period calculates. The chain runs one way: prior debt → interest → net income → cash → this period's debt. No loop.

Average-balance interest reads this period's closing debt, which hasn't been determined yet: closing debt depends on the sweep, the sweep depends on cash available, cash available depends on net income, and net income depends on the interest you are trying to calculate. The formula is an input to itself. Excel detects the loop and returns 0 unless iterative calculation is enabled, at which point it guesses, recalculates, and repeats until the change between passes falls under the tolerance.

Ratio Analysis · core

DuPont pyramid (ROE decomposition)

ROE is a single number that three completely different stories can produce. DuPont exists to force those stories apart: margin, efficiency, and leverage, so "returns went up" turns into "returns went up because of what."

You already have the definitions (ROE = net income ÷ equity, developed by DuPont's finance department in the 1920s to manage the conglomerate's internal divisions). The part worth building real fluency in is the algebra of how it splits, because each split isolates a different lever a management team — or an acquirer — actually pulls, and each version of the pyramid is used for a different diagnostic job on the desk.

01The 2-lever split — why the algebra is the whole point

Start where the course did:

RatioFormulaWhat it isolates
Return on assets (ROA)Net income ÷ Total assetsProfit generated per dollar of assets deployed — operating performance, leverage-blind
Equity multiplierTotal assets ÷ Shareholders' equityHow much of the asset base is financed by debt rather than equity

ROE = ROA × Equity multiplier = (NI ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity) = NI ÷ Equity

Careful with the direction: the multiplier is Assets ÷ Equity, not Equity ÷ Assets. It answers "how many dollars of assets does each dollar of equity support?" — so a higher multiplier means equity is doing less of the financing, not more. A company with $40 of equity funding $100 of assets has a 2.5× multiplier and an equity-financed share of 40% (60% debt-financed); shrink equity to $20 against the same $100 of assets and the multiplier rises to 5× while the equity-financed share falls to 20%. Multiplier up = equity share down = leverage up.

The reason this is worth dwelling on isn't the cancellation trick — it's what the cancellation proves: ROE is mathematically inseparable from how levered the business is. A company can hold ROA flat and still push ROE up simply by financing more of its asset base with debt. That single fact is the reason nobody serious quotes ROE without also knowing what's driving it — it's the most gameable "good number" in ratio analysis.

02The 3-lever (classic) pyramid

Split ROA itself into margin and efficiency, and you get the version actually called "the DuPont analysis":

ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier
   = (NI ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity)

LeverAnswersMoved by
Net marginHow much profit survives per dollar of sales?Pricing power, cost discipline, mix
Asset turnoverHow much revenue per dollar of assets?Inventory/receivables efficiency, capital intensity, asset-light vs. asset-heavy model
Equity multiplierHow much of the balance sheet is debt-financed?Capital structure choice, not operations

The reason this three-way split earns its place in the curriculum is that it explains why two companies can post the same ROE for opposite reasons — and why comparing ROE alone across industries is close to meaningless:

BusinessNet marginAsset turnoverEquity mult.≈ ROE
Luxury goods house22%0.7×1.9×29%
Grocery retailer2.5%3.2×3.6×29%
Regional bank18%0.06×10.0×≈11%*

*Banks run turnover on total assets, which is structurally tiny for a balance sheet that's mostly loans — this is exactly why ROE for financials is normally read against equity/tangible equity, not decomposed via the standard operating pyramid.

The luxury house earns its return through margin on very few units. The grocer earns the identical return by moving huge volume through thin margins, fast. Same ROE, opposite business quality, opposite operating risk if volumes stall. That's the diagnostic DuPont is built to surface — never accept a headline ROE without asking which lever is carrying it.

03The 5-step (extended) pyramid — the version practitioners actually use

The classic 3-step pyramid still bundles two different things inside "net margin": how the business performs operationally, and how its capital structure and tax situation affect what reaches the bottom line. The extended pyramid pulls those apart:

ROE = Tax burden × Interest burden × Operating margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier

LeverFormulaIsolates
Tax burdenNI ÷ EBTHow much of pre-tax profit survives taxation — the tax rate/jurisdiction/planning effect
Interest burdenEBT ÷ EBITHow much operating profit is eaten by financing cost — the leverage/capital-structure effect
Operating marginEBIT ÷ RevenuePure operating profitability — before financing and tax decisions touch it
Asset turnoverRevenue ÷ AssetsSame as before — asset efficiency
Equity multiplierAssets ÷ EquitySame as before — financial leverage
Why this is the version that matters for your career

The 5-step split strips financing and tax noise off operating performance — two companies can show very different ROE for reasons that have nothing to do with how well either business runs.

  • Operating margin & turnover — identical. That's the real business.
  • Interest burden, tax burden, equity multiplier — the entire ~37pt gap. Financing and jurisdiction, not skill.
  • Same reflex → LBO IRR bridge: EBITDA growth = operating · multiple expansion = market · deleveraging = financing (mirrors equity multiplier: EV = Debt+Equity, pay debt down while EV holds flat → equity's slice grows mechanically)
  • Rule: never credit a return to "the business got better" until financing's share is subtracted out.

04Reading a DuPont bridge — the actual skill

The exercise that matters isn't computing the five ratios for one year — it's walking the bridge across two years or across a company and its comp set, and naming which lever moved.

Drill · A company's ROE rose from 14% to 19% year over year. Operating margin and asset turnover are both flat. What happened, and should you be pleased?

With operating margin and turnover unchanged, the increase has to be coming from the interest burden, the tax burden, or the equity multiplier — i.e., from financing or tax, not operations. The most common real-world cause is the equity multiplier: the company levered up (took on more debt, or bought back stock, shrinking the equity base) and the same operating profit now sits on top of a smaller equity denominator.

Should you be pleased? Not automatically. The business is not more profitable or more efficient than it was — it is more financially levered, which raises ROE mechanically while also raising financial risk (fixed interest obligations, less cushion in a downturn). This is exactly the pattern a quality-of-earnings review is built to catch, and exactly the question a good interviewer is fishing for when they ask "ROE went up — is that good news?" The correct answer starts with "it depends which lever moved," not "yes."

Where DuPont breaks down — the limitations worth knowing before you cite it
  • Negative or near-zero equity makes ROE meaningless. A company can buy back stock aggressively enough to shrink equity toward zero or negative; ROE then explodes or flips sign with no change in the underlying business. Always sanity-check the equity multiplier itself before trusting the headline number.
  • It's a snapshot ratio built on book values, not market or intrinsic value. Two identical operating businesses can show different ROE purely from different accounting histories — asset write-downs, older vs. newer PP&E, goodwill from a past acquisition sitting on one balance sheet and not the other.
  • It says nothing about whether the return clears the cost of capital. A leveraged ROE of 19% funded by expensive debt can destroy value even while the ratio looks better than an unlevered 14% funded entirely by equity. DuPont tells you how a return was produced; it takes WACC and a cost-of-capital comparison (coming later in the queue) to tell you whether that return was actually worth having.
  • Off-balance-sheet leverage distorts the equity multiplier. Operating leases used to sit off-balance-sheet entirely before ASC 842 / IFRS 16 brought them on; comparing DuPont pyramids across companies reporting under different standards, or across periods straddling that transition, can quietly compare different definitions of "assets."
Bottom line

ROE tells you the size of a return. DuPont tells you whether to trust it — by breaking one ambiguous number into margin, efficiency, and leverage so you can tell genuine operating quality apart from financial engineering, compare companies across different capital structures and tax jurisdictions, and price the risk a headline ROE hides. Every example above — the buyback, the levered-vs-unlevered comparison, the LBO parallel — is that one idea applied to a different situation.

"I thought the equity multiplier was about how much of the asset base is financed by equity?"

Close, but the direction is inverted. The multiplier is Assets ÷ Equity — dollars of assets per dollar of equity — and it rises as equity finances a smaller share of the balance sheet, not a larger one.

What you were picturing has a name: the equity ratio, Equity ÷ Assets — the actual percentage of the asset base financed by equity. It's the exact reciprocal of the multiplier (multiplier = 1 ÷ equity ratio). $40 equity funding $100 of assets is a 40% equity ratio and a 2.5× multiplier — same fact, two ways of stating it, moving in opposite directions as leverage changes. Shrink equity to $20 against the same $100 of assets: equity ratio falls to 20%, multiplier rises to 5×.

Why DuPont uses the multiplier form and not the ratio form: because ROE = ROA × ROA's-leverage-adjustment needs something that multiplies ROA up as leverage rises, and Assets÷Equity does that (values >1, growing with leverage). Equity÷Assets would need to divide, which doesn't cancel cleanly in the algebra. The multiplier is the form that makes the identity work — worth knowing it's leverage expressed as "how many times the equity is levered up," not "what percentage is equity."

"Explain more: why is ROE 'mathematically inseparable' from leverage, and why does that make it the most gameable ratio?"

Two different things are being said in that line, worth separating. "The cancellation trick" is just the algebra — Assets cancels out of ROA × Equity multiplier, leaving NI÷Equity. True by construction, not really an insight. What the cancellation proves is the identity read the other direction: ROE = ROA × Leverage. That's not a correlation, it's a definition — every ROE number you will ever see is structurally built from an operating piece and a financing piece, whether anyone bothers to split it or not. "Mathematically inseparable" means exactly that: you cannot state a company's ROE without it having implicitly encoded how levered that company is.

The worked version of "hold ROA flat, push ROE up":

AssetsNet incomeROAEquityDebtMultiplierROE
A$100$88%$100$01.0×8%
B$100$88%$50$502.0×16%
C$100$88%$20$805.0×40%

Same assets, same profit, same ROA — by any operating measure the business did the identical thing in all three rows. The only variable that moved is how the $100 of assets is financed. ROE went from 8% to 40%: a 5x'd headline number produced with zero operating improvement, purely by shrinking the equity base and replacing it with debt. (In reality more debt usually adds interest expense, which would pull NI and ROA down slightly — held flat here on purpose, to isolate the multiplier's effect in isolation from that second-order drag.)

Told as a story instead of a table: GreenLeaf Foods has $500M of assets, $40M of net income (8% ROA), split $250M equity / $250M debt (2.0× multiplier, 16% ROE). The board wants a better ROE this year, so instead of improving margins or moving inventory faster, they borrow $150M and use the cash to buy back $150M of stock. Trace the transaction: debt rises to $400M, equity falls to $100M (shares retired), and assets end up unchanged at $500M — the borrowed cash came in and went straight back out to fund the buyback, so it nets to zero on the balance sheet. Result: same $500M of assets, same $40M of profit, same 8% ROA — but the multiplier is now 5.0× and ROE is 40%. Read as a headline, "ROE nearly tripled" sounds like the business got much better. It didn't — the balance sheet got more levered. That gap between the headline and the operating reality is exactly what decomposing ROE is for.

That's the gameability: a CFO who wants a better-looking ROE doesn't need to improve the business at all — issue debt, buy back stock, shrink the equity denominator, done. Compare that to ROIC (return on invested capital) or plain ROA, which sit on top of the asset base rather than equity — you can't move those with a buyback, because the denominator doesn't shrink when equity does. That asymmetry is exactly why ROE is the one "good number" that gets manipulated and the others don't get quoted in isolation either.

And it's exactly the mechanism an LBO runs on, deliberately rather than as a red flag: a PE firm financing a deal mostly with debt is engineering a high equity multiplier on purpose, so the same dollar of enterprise-value growth produces a much bigger percentage return on a much smaller equity check. The reflex this builds — always asking "did the business get better, or did the equity just get smaller?" — is the same reflex you'll use reading comps and building a returns bridge later.
